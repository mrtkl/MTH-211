Chapter 5 overview

Chapter 5Topics Primes, Composites and Tests for Divisibility · Counting Factors, GCF and LCM

Big idea. Primes are the building blocks of the whole numbers under multiplication: every composite number is a product of primes in exactly one way (the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic). Divisibility tests, factor trees, the GCF and the LCM are all ways of reading that prime structure.

Why it matters for teachers. Children meet factors and multiples in arrays and skip counting, and they use the GCF and LCM later for simplifying fractions and finding common denominators. Teachers need to explain why the divisibility tests work (place value), not just state them.

Figures and worked examples. Items marked New add figures (arrays, factor trees, base ten pieces, Venn diagrams and number lines), worked examples and classification tasks that parallel the chapter, with new numbers and contexts.

How to use this file. The notes pages explain the content with manipulatives you can project. Each activity page lists the handout’s questions in order, with buttons that open the matching tool already set up. Instructor notes are in the teal “Teaching notes” boxes.

Chapter 5 Notes (1): Primes, Composites and Divisibility

Leads to Activity 5.2

Terminology

Let a and b be whole numbers with a ≠ 0. If there is a whole number x with a · x = b, then:

  • a is a factor (or divisor) of b, and b is a multiple of a;
  • we say a divides b and write a | b. If a does not divide b, we write a ∤ b.
  • The divisors of b are all of its factors: the divisors of 12 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 12.
  • A prime is a counting number with exactly two different factors, 1 and itself: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, …
  • A composite number has more than two factors: 4, 6, 8, 9, … The number 1 is neither prime nor composite (it has only one factor).

In an array model, a | b exactly when b objects can be arranged in a rectangle with a rows and no objects left over.

The Sieve of Eratosthenes

Choose a prime, then cross out its multiples. The numbers never crossed out are the primes.

Important theorems

The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic. Each composite number can be expressed as a product of primes in exactly one way (except for the order of the factors). Example: 360 = 2 · 2 · 2 · 3 · 3 · 5 = 2³ · 3² · 5, and every factor tree for 360 ends with these same primes.
The Prime Factor Test. To test for prime factors of a number n, one only needs to search for prime factors p with p² ≤ n. If no such prime divides n, then n is prime.
Divisibility by a product. A number is divisible by the product ab of two whole numbers a and b if it is divisible by both a and b, and a and b have only 1 as a common factor. Example: divisible by 2 and 3 ⇒ divisible by 6. But 12 is divisible by 4 and 6 and not by 24, since 4 and 6 share the factor 2.
Three divisibility facts. If a, m, n and k are whole numbers and a ≠ 0:
1. If a | m and a | n, then a | (m + n).
2. If a | m and a | n, then a | (m − n) for m > n.
3. If a | m, then a | km.

Worked example: prime or composite? New
  • 323: 17² = 289 ≤ 323 < 361 = 19², so check primes 2 through 17. 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 and 13 fail, but 323 = 17 × 19: composite.

Worked example: prime factorization with the tests New

Factor 3,564. The digit sum is 18, so 9 | 3,564 and 3,564 = 9 × 396. Since 4 | 96, 396 = 4 × 99. And 99 = 9 × 11. So 3,564 = 9 × 4 × 9 × 11 = 2² × 3⁴ × 11.

Divisible or not? New

Use the tests, and watch the condition for divisibility by a product: the two factors may share only 1.

Teaching notes: Notes (1)
  • Note on the prime factor test: the handout states the condition as p² < n. It must be p² ≤ n: for 49, the only prime factor is 7, and 7² = 49. The tool here uses p² ≤ n.
  • Ask why 1 is not prime. With 1 as a prime, factorizations are no longer unique (6 = 2 · 3 = 1 · 2 · 3 = 1 · 1 · 2 · 3), which is exactly what students see when 1 is in the cup in Activity 5.1.
  • Divisibility by a product needs the “only 1 in common” condition; the 12, 4, 6, 24 example is the standard counterexample.
  • New Model the test for 3 with base ten pieces: break one unit off each flat and each long. The rest is groups of 99 and 9; the broken-off units are the digit sum.
  • New Ask students to draw three different factor trees for the same number before stating the Fundamental Theorem; the identical leaves make the theorem feel inevitable.
  • New The crossover table for 211 explains the prime factor test: once the prime passes the quotient, any factor pair would already have been found.

Counting factors New

Every factor of n is built from the primes of n, each used at most as often as in n. If n = p1a × p2b × …, then n has (a + 1)(b + 1)… factors: each exponent can be 0, 1, …, up to its value.

72 = 2³ × 3²: 4 choices for the power of 2, 3 for the power of 3, so 4 × 3 = 12 factors.

Worked examples: how many factors? New
  • 200 = 2³ × 5²: (3 + 1)(2 + 1) = 12 factors.
  • 180 = 2² × 3² × 5: (2 + 1)(2 + 1)(1 + 1) = 18 factors.
  • 2⁵ × 7³ × 11: 6 × 4 × 2 = 48 factors.
  • 8⁴ × 3: first rewrite 8⁴ = 2¹², since 8 is not prime. Then 13 × 2 = 26 factors.
  • The count depends only on the exponents, not on which primes appear.

Greatest common factor (GCF)

Definition. The greatest common factor of two (or more) nonzero whole numbers is the greatest whole number that divides each of them. We write GCF(a, b).
Example. Factors of 24: {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24}. Factors of 36: {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36}. Common factors: {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12}, so GCF(24, 36) = 12.

Finding the GCF:

  • Listing (the set intersection method): list the factors of each number and take the largest number in the intersection.
  • Prime factorization method: 24 = 2³ · 3 and 36 = 2² · 3². Take each common prime to the smaller power: GCF = 2² · 3 = 12.
  • Euclidean algorithm: this method is optional; you will not be required to use it. GCF(a, b) = GCF(b, r), where r is the remainder of a ÷ b.
Worked example: GCF(60, 84) two ways New
  • Set intersection: factors of 60 = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, 60}; factors of 84 = {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 12, 14, 21, 28, 42, 84}. Common: {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12}. GCF = 12.
  • Prime factorization: 60 = 2² × 3 × 5 and 84 = 2² × 3 × 7. Common primes, smaller exponents: 2² × 3 = 12. The 5 and the 7 are not shared, so they are left out.
For reading: two GCF shortcuts New
  • Subtraction (GCF(a, b) = GCF(a − b, b)): GCF(462, 330) = GCF(132, 330) = GCF(132, 198) = GCF(132, 66) = GCF(66, 66) = 66.
  • Euclidean algorithm (divide, keep the remainder): 4,158 = 2 × 1,512 + 1,134; 1,512 = 1 × 1,134 + 378; 1,134 = 3 × 378 + 0. The last nonzero remainder is 378 = GCF(4,158, 1,512).

Least common multiple (LCM)

Definition. The least common multiple of two (or more) nonzero whole numbers is the smallest nonzero whole number that is a multiple of each of them. We write LCM(a, b).
Example. Multiples of 6: {6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, …}. Multiples of 8: {8, 16, 24, 32, …}. The first common multiple is 24, so LCM(6, 8) = 24.

Finding the LCM:

  • Listing (set intersection): list multiples of each and take the smallest common one.
  • Build-up method (relies on prime factorization): start with the prime factorization of one number and multiply in just the extra primes the other needs. 6 = 2 · 3; 8 = 2³ needs two more 2s: LCM = 2 · 3 · 2 · 2 = 24.
  • With prime factorizations: take each prime to the larger power: LCM(24, 36) = 2³ · 3² = 72.

Useful fact: GCF(a, b) · LCM(a, b) = a · b.

Worked example: LCM(45, 60) three ways New
  • Set intersection: {45, 90, 135, 180, …} ∩ {60, 120, 180, …} = {180, 360, …}; the smallest is 180.
  • Prime factorization: 45 = 3² × 5, 60 = 2² × 3 × 5. Every prime, larger exponent: 2² × 3² × 5 = 180.
  • Build-up: start with 60 = 2² × 3 × 5; 45 needs one more 3. LCM = 2² × 3² × 5 = 180.
Worked examples: three numbers, and GCF × LCM New
  • GCF(18, 30, 42): 18 = 2 × 3², 30 = 2 × 3 × 5, 42 = 2 × 3 × 7, so GCF = 2 × 3 = 6 and LCM = 2 × 3² × 5 × 7 = 630.
  • Or two at a time: GCF(18, 30) = 6, then GCF(6, 42) = 6.
  • LCM(28, 42): GCF(28, 42) = 14, so LCM = (28 × 42) ÷ 14 = 2 × 42 = 84. This shortcut works for two numbers only.

Venn diagrams with prime factorization

Venn diagrams can be used together with prime factorization to find both the GCF and the LCM. The shared primes go in the overlap.

GCF or LCM? New

Splitting things into equal groups or pieces calls for the GCF; waiting for things to line up calls for the LCM.

There are infinitely many primes New

It can be proved indirectly. Suppose 2, 3, 5, …, p were all the primes, and let N = (2 × 3 × 5 × … × p) + 1. Dividing N by any prime on the list leaves remainder 1, so no listed prime divides N. Then N would be neither prime nor composite, which is impossible.

Try it with real numbers New
  • 2 × 3 × 5 × 7 + 1 = 211, which is prime (see Notes (1)).
  • 2 × 3 × 5 × 7 × 11 × 13 + 1 = 30,031 = 59 × 509. It is not prime, but its prime factors, 59 and 509, are new primes not on the list. Either way the list was incomplete.

All the methods side by side

Teaching notes: Notes (2)
  • The Venn method shows both answers at once: GCF is the product of the overlap; LCM is the product of everything in the diagram.
  • Have students check GCF · LCM = a · b; it is a quick way to catch errors.
  • Listing is how children start; the prime-factorization methods are what make large numbers manageable.
  • New Use the factor-count grid for 72 to show that the factors pair up (1 and 72, 2 and 36, …); a number has an odd number of factors exactly when it is a perfect square. This explains the classic locker problem.
  • New Students often believe N = 2 × 3 × … × p + 1 is always prime. The example 30,031 = 59 × 509 shows the proof does not need that.
  • New In word problems ask “groups or line up?” before computing; the classification task gives six contexts to practise.

Activity 5.1: Big Bag O’ Numbers

Notes Notes (1): primes, Fundamental TheoremManipulative magic cup, number chips

Materials: a magic cup and chips numbered 1–10 (4 of each). Read the rules carefully.

  1. One player blindly draws exactly 2, 3 or 4 chips, multiplies the numbers, and tells the others only the product.
  2. The others secretly write a guess for (a) how many chips were drawn and (b) which numbers. After everyone shares, the chips are revealed.
  3. Scoring: correct number of chips, 1 point; correct numbers too, 2 more points (3 total). If nobody guesses both exactly, the drawer gets 5 points.
  4. Play passes around; play 1 round and tally scores.

Part One uses all 40 chips (1–10). Part Two uses only the prime chips. Discuss: Is there strategy? Are some guesses more likely to be right? Why does Part Two feel different?

Play the game

Analyze a product

For any product, list every way it could have been drawn from the cup. Compare the two cups, with and without the 1 chips.

Questions to ponder

  • What differences did you notice between the games? Formulate conjectures and justify them.
  • Why does it work like it does? Did your group include the chips labeled 1? What happens if 1 is included as a prime?
  • Can you explain to a peer why primes behave this way?
Show a summary

With chips 1–10, many products can be made in several ways (24 = 3 · 8 = 4 · 6 = 2 · 3 · 4 = 2 · 2 · 6 …), so guessing is a gamble and the drawer often wins. With only primes, every product breaks into primes in exactly one way, so a careful guesser can always find the chips: this is the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic. If the 1 chips are included, uniqueness fails again for the number of chips (6 = 2 · 3 = 1 · 2 · 3), which is one reason 1 is not called prime.

Teaching notes: Activity 5.1
  • Do not turn to Part Two until the discussion of Part One has produced the idea that some products are “ambiguous.”
  • The analyzer shows the number of possible draws for any product; try 24, 36, 60 and 72 in Part One versus the same idea in Part Two.
  • Probability aside: in Part One some products (such as 7 · 7 · 7 = 343) are unambiguous because 7 is prime and the only chip with a factor of 7.

Teaching notes: Activity 5.2
  • By 2: 16 yes, 19 no, 103 no. Every long and flat splits into groups of 2 exactly, so only the units can leave a remainder.
  • By 4: 32 yes, 132 yes, 126 no, 360 yes; 732 yes, 526 no, 21,516 yes. Each flat (100 = 25 · 4) and larger piece splits into groups of 4, but a long (10) does not, so the last two digits matter.
  • By 8: 1111 is not (each 1000 splits into 125 eights, a flat leaves 4, a long leaves 2, a unit leaves 1; 4 + 2 + 1 = 7). 1456 yes, 4343 no, 234 no, 10000 yes, 8886 no.
  • By 9: each flat leaves 1 unit (100 = 99 + 1), each long leaves 1 (10 = 9 + 1), each unit leaves 1, so the leftovers equal the digit sum. 81 yes, 128 no (leftover 11 → 1 more group, 2 units), 297 yes (31 groups shaded, 18 leftovers = 2 more groups). 36,495: 3 + 6 + 4 + 9 + 5 = 27, divisible by 9, so yes.

Activity 5.3: Greatest Common Factors and Least Common Multiples

Notes Notes (1), (2)Manipulative objects, factor trees, gears

Part 1: Finding factors

  1. List all factors of 72 systematically, pairing from the outside in (1 with 72, 2 with 36, …). When can you stop checking? Why?
  2. Katrina finds the prime factorization of 10395 with divisibility tests. a. What test first? b. What next? c. Complete the tree, describing every try.

Part 2: LCM and GCF

  1. A teacher shows only LCM(3, 10) and LCM(8, 9). What misconception might students develop? What other examples should they see?
  2. With 16 objects: a. show all the factors of 16; b. with 20 more objects, find GCF(16, 20).
  3. Sandy’s pencils (packages of 18) and erasers (packages of 24): smallest number of each so every pencil gets an eraser? How many packages? LCM or GCF?
  4. Karen’s 45 red and 30 green candies: largest number of identical gift bags using all the candy? How many of each per bag? LCM or GCF?
  5. Use the build-up or Venn diagram method: a. GCF and LCM of 54, 126; b. of 84, 98; c. of 27, 84, 98.
  6. Meshed gears with 35 and 15 teeth, stars aligned: how many turns of each until the stars align again? LCM or GCF?
  7. If a is a factor of b, what is GCF(a, b)? LCM(a, b)? Explain.
  8. When is LCM(a, b) = a × b? Explain.
Teaching notes: Activity 5.3
  • Factors of 72: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12, 18, 24, 36, 72. Stop once the next number to test passes the partner you just found (after 8 · 9, the next test, 10, is larger than √72 ≈ 8.5).
  • 10395 ends in 5, so try 5 first: 5 · 2079. Digit sum 2 + 0 + 7 + 9 = 18, so 9 | 2079: 9 · 231 = 3 · 3 · 3 · 7 · 11. Result: 3³ · 5 · 7 · 11.
  • Part 2: #1 students may think LCM(a, b) is always a · b; show LCM(4, 6) = 12 and LCM(6, 8) = 24. #3 LCM(18, 24) = 72: 4 packages of pencils and 3 of erasers. #4 GCF(45, 30) = 15 bags with 3 red and 2 green each.
  • #5: a. GCF 18, LCM 378; b. GCF 14, LCM 588; c. GCF 1, LCM 5292 (2² · 3³ · 7²). #6: LCM(35, 15) = 105 teeth: the large gear turns 3 times and the small gear 7 times.
  • #7: GCF(a, b) = a and LCM(a, b) = b. #8: exactly when GCF(a, b) = 1 (a and b are relatively prime).

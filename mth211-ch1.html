Big idea. Problem solving is the heart of mathematics and of this course. Polya’s four steps (understand, plan, carry out, look back) give a structure; the strategies (guess and test, draw a picture, use a variable, look for a pattern, make a list, solve a simpler problem) give the tools. The habit we build all term is explaining reasoning so that a class of children could follow it.

How to use this file. The notes pages explain the content with manipulatives you can project. Each activity page keeps the handout’s questions in order and adds a manipulative: tiles that grow, toothpick houses, dot arrays, a checkerboard counter, a cryptarithm tester.

Chapter 1 Notes (1): The Problem-Solving Process

Read pp. 1–17Leads to Activity 1.1

The authors of our text distinguish between exercises and problems:

Exercise. A task that can be done by applying a known procedure; it is routine practice. Example: 347 + 586.
Problem. A task for which the solver does not have a ready-made method; it requires thinking and choosing a strategy. Example: Find two whole numbers whose sum is 30 and whose product is 216.

Whether a task is an exercise or a problem depends on the solver: what is a problem for a second grader may be an exercise for an adult.

Exercise or problem?

Polya’s four steps

George Polya, author of How to Solve It, identified four distinct phases of successful problem solving:

  1. Understand the Problem (UTP): What is the unknown? What are the data and conditions? Restate the problem in your own words, list the important details, and clarify vocabulary.
  2. Devise a Plan (DAP): Choose a strategy (or several). Have you seen a similar problem? Which clues in the problem suggest a strategy?
  3. Carry out the Plan (COP): Implement the strategy, showing and explaining every step. If it is not working, go back and change the plan.
  4. Look Back (LB): Check the answer in the original problem. Does it make sense? Are there other solutions? Is there another method, a generalization or an extension?

Sort the moves into Polya’s steps

For each thing a problem solver does, choose the step it belongs to.

Chapter 1 Notes (2): How Do I Devise a Plan?

Read pp. 1–17Strategies 1–3

How do we know when to try a particular strategy? Each strategy has clues: features of a problem that suggest it may help.

Strategy 1: Guess and test

Clues: there is a limited number of possibilities to test; you want to understand the problem better; you have a good idea what the answer might be; the problem involves several conditions that are hard to satisfy at once.

Three types of guess and test:

  • Random: guesses with no particular order or reason.
  • Systematic: guesses made in an organized way (a table, in order) so nothing is repeated or missed.
  • Inferential: guesses chosen by reasoning, so many possibilities are ruled out without trying them.

Example: Find two whole numbers whose sum is 30 and whose product is 216. Try it three ways below.

Strategy 2: Draw a picture

Clues: a physical situation is involved; geometric figures or measurements are involved; you want to understand the problem better; a visual representation is possible.

Example: A fence is 100 feet long with a post every 10 feet, including both ends. How many posts? Many people answer 10; the picture shows 11.

Strategy 3: Use a variable

Clues: a phrase like “for any number”; a problem that suggests an equation; a proof or general statement is called for; there are unknowns; you are trying to generalize a pattern.

Example: “Think of a number. Add 5. Double it. Subtract 4. Divide by 2. Subtract the number you started with.” The result is always 3. A variable explains why: with n, ((n + 5) · 2 − 4) ÷ 2 − n = (2n + 6) ÷ 2 − n = n + 3 − n = 3.

Top 10 bad habits for problem solving

  1. Trying to do it all in your head, not writing anything down.
  2. Arbitrarily choosing a strategy.
  3. Staying with a strategy when it’s not working.
  4. Giving up on a strategy too early.
  5. Getting fixated on a single strategy and trying to use it for everything.
  6. Not asking yourself: “Does this make sense?”
  7. Being afraid to ask for help.
  8. Not checking your answer.
  9. Not noticing patterns.
  10. Going through the motions instead of thinking.

Top 10 ways to get unstuck

  1. Reread the problem.
  2. Modify your strategy.
  3. Change your strategy.
  4. Combine your strategy with another strategy.
  5. Look at the problem from a new perspective.
  6. Look at the answer.
  7. Look at other similar problems.
  8. Get help.
  9. Wait awhile and try again.
  10. All of the above.

Chapter 1 Notes (3): More Strategies

Read pp. 21–31Strategies 4–6

Strategy 4: Look for a pattern

Clues: a list of data is given; a sequence of numbers or figures is involved; listing special cases helps; you are asked to generalize or find the nth term.

Example: Find the next terms and the nth term of 4, 7, 10, 13, … Each term is 3 more than the one before: the nth term is 3n + 1.

Strategy 5: Make a list

Clues: information can easily be organized; data can easily be presented in a table; the problem asks “how many ways”; you are looking for all possible answers.

Example: In how many ways can you make 25 cents with pennies, nickels, dimes and quarters? A systematic list, starting with the largest coin, finds all 13 ways.

Strategy 6: Solve a simpler problem

Clues: the problem involves large numbers; a very complex problem; a more general problem could be solved more easily.

Example: Find 1 + 2 + 3 + … + 100. Try 1 + … + 4 and 1 + … + 10 first: pairing first and last gives (n/2) pairs of (n + 1), so the sum is 100 · 101 ÷ 2 = 5050.

Combining strategies

In many problem-solving situations you will need a combination of strategies. Example: how many squares of all sizes are on an 8 × 8 checkerboard? Solve a simpler problem (1 × 1, 2 × 2, 3 × 3 boards), draw pictures, make a list of squares by size, then look for a pattern: the totals 1, 5, 14, 30 are sums of square numbers. See Activity 1.9.

Activity 1.1: Practicing Polya’s Four Steps

Notes Notes (1)Tool write-up checklist, explorers

Part 1: Using Polya’s four steps effectively

Understand the Problem: a bulleted list of all the important details (do not just restate the problem), plus the clarifications you, as a teacher, would make so all students understand the vocabulary and mathematics.

Devise a Plan: a short general statement of the strategy or strategies you will use; no steps of work.

Carry Out the Plan: show all work clearly and completely; describe in words what you are doing and why it makes sense; finish by answering the question in a complete sentence.

Look Back: check your answer and show the work; explain why the answer makes sense in context; check whether there is more than one solution, and find them all or explain why there is only one. You may also discuss changes to your plan, patterns, generalizations or extensions.

Part 2, Problem 1: The book

A student opens a 125-page book and announces, “The sum of the two facing page numbers is a square number.” What are the page numbers the student has opened to?

Part 2, Problem 2: Raffle tickets

Mariella is selling raffle tickets for a school fundraiser. Adult tickets cost $4 and student tickets cost $3. If she has collected $65 so far, how many adult tickets did she sell?

Alternative solution: two constant center tiles, three growing arms

Look at the two tiles in the center of the T: the corner tile where the bar meets the stem, and the tile just below it. They are in every T, so they are the constant part. Everything else is three arms (left, right and down), and each arm has exactly n tiles in the T of Size n.

So the T of Size n has 2 + n + n + n = 3n + 2 tiles, the same formula as the top-bar-and-stem view. Choose “Two center tiles + three arms” in the color coding above to see it on any size: the red tiles never change, and each colored arm grows by one tile from one size to the next. This view also explains why the T grows by 3 tiles each time: one tile is added to each of the three arms.

  1. Draw the next two T’s in the sequence.
  2. Describe in words how the tile T’s are growing.
  3. What does the T of Size 10 look like? How many tiles? How do you know?
  4. Describe in words, a sketch, and a formula a rule for the number of tiles in a T of Size n. Use color to connect the formula to the drawing.
  5. With 47 tiles, can you make a T that uses all of them? What Size?
  6. What is the largest Size T you could make with 145 tiles?
  7. Describe any rules for numbers of tiles that can or cannot make tile T’s.

Activity 1.4: Looking for and Expressing Patterns: Even and Odd Numbers

Notes Notes (2)–(3)Manipulative square tiles in two rows

Part 1: Two rows of tiles

1. Represent the counting numbers with tiles, then arrange each pile in two horizontal rows that are equal or as close to equal as possible. 2. Which numbers make two equal rows? Describe the 6th, 10th and nth such number. 3. Describe the numbers that cannot, as “one row of ___ tiles and another row of ___ tiles.” 4. Recap the nth even and nth odd number. 5. Is 0 even or odd?

Part 2: Generalizing sums

Make and justify (with tiles and symbols) conjectures about 1. even + even, 2. odd + odd, 3. even + odd.

Part 3: Consecutive numbers and multiples of 3

  1. Is the sum of two consecutive whole numbers always odd, always even, or sometimes each? Justify.
  2. Can every odd number be written as a sum of two consecutive whole numbers?
  3. Multiples of 3: a. draw tile pictures (three equal rows); b. show the sum of three consecutive whole numbers is always a multiple of 3; c. show every nonzero multiple of 3 is a sum of three consecutive whole numbers; d. how are b and c different?

Activity 1.7: Cryptarithm

Notes Notes (1)–(2): Polya, guess and testTool cryptarithm tester

Part 1. Find digits A, B, C and D that make the multiplication true:

ABCD × 4 = DCBA

Write out your solution using Polya’s four steps: label and show all four steps, show all work in the order you did it, and explain each step of reasoning in words.

Try digits

Inferential guess and test: one clue at a time

Part 2: Reflecting on your approach

Answer the questions for the approach your group started with, then for any approach you used later.

  • Random guess and test (choices for no specific reason): Did you modify your approach after a few tries? What discoveries narrowed (or could have narrowed) the digits to check? What would you change now?
  • Systematic guess and check (tries tracked carefully, nothing repeated): Same three questions.
  • Inferential guess and test (ideas about how numbers work rule things out without trying them): What assumptions did you start with? What discoveries narrowed the digits after your first tries? What would you change now?

Activity 1.8: More Problem Solving: Looking for and Expressing Patterns

Notes Notes (3)Manipulative dot arrays, handshake diagram

1. Rectangular numbers

The rectangular numbers are 2, 6, 12, 20, … a) 6th? b) 10th? c) What pattern exists in the diagrams? d) A formula for the nth. e) The 100th.

2. Triangular numbers

The triangular numbers are 1, 3, 6, 10, … a) 6th? b) 10th? c) How are they similar to and different from the rectangular numbers? d) A formula for the nth (find more than one way). e) The 100th.

3. Handshakes

Each group member shakes hands with each other member exactly once. How many handshakes for a) 4 people, b) 8, c) 1, d) 10? e) A formula for n people (more than one way). f) 100 people.

Wrap-up discussion

  1. Did the pattern in question 1 help with question 2? Why or why not?
  2. Did the pattern in question 2 help with question 3? Why or why not?
  3. Did you write the formula in question 2 in at least two ways? Which was easier to explain, and which to use?
  4. The same for question 3.

Chapter 4 overview

Textbook Musser et al. 10e, Ch. 4Sections 4.1 Mental Math, Estimation and Calculators · 4.2 Written Algorithms · 4.3 Algorithms in Other Bases

Big idea. Every computation method, whether mental, estimated or written, works because of place value and the properties of the operations. Mental math techniques are the properties used deliberately; written algorithms are place value and the distributive property written compactly. Our focus throughout is not how to do each method but why it works.

How to use this file. The notes pages explain the content with manipulatives you can project. Each activity page lists the handout’s questions in order, with buttons that open the matching tool already set up, and answer checks.

Chapter 4 Notes (1): Mental Math and Estimation

Read pp. 128–140Leads to Activities 4.1, 4.2

Review of properties of arithmetic

Properties of addition and multiplication
PropertyOf additionOf multiplication
Identitya + 0 = 0 + a = aa × 1 = 1 × a = a
Commutativea + b = b + aa × b = b × a
Associativea + (b + c) = (a + b) + ca × (b × c) = (a × b) × c
Distributive (multiplication over addition or subtraction)a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c  and  a × (b − c) = a × b − a × c

Mental math techniques (exact answers)

All of these techniques rely on the properties of arithmetic. The text shows how to use them; in class we go deeper and name exactly how and where each property is used.

  1. Compensation uses properties to change to an equivalent problem with compatible numbers:
    • Additive compensation: 98 + 59 = 98 + (2 + 57) = (98 + 2) + 57 = 100 + 57 = 157 (associative).
    • Equal additions method (subtraction): 43 − 28 = (43 + 2) − (28 + 2) = 45 − 30 = 15. Adding the same amount to both numbers keeps the difference.
    • Multiplicative compensation: 48 × 5 = (24 × 2) × 5 = 24 × (2 × 5) = 24 × 10 = 240 (associative); “halving and doubling.”
  2. Left-to-right methods: 47 + 36 = (40 + 30) + (7 + 6) = 70 + 13 = 83, working with the largest place first.
  3. Multiplying powers of 10: 300 × 4000 = (3 × 4) × (100 × 1000) = 12 × 100,000 = 1,200,000.
  4. Multiplying by special factors: × 5 = × 10 ÷ 2; × 25 = × 100 ÷ 4; × 99 = × 100 − × 1 (distributive).

Computational estimation (approximate answers)

  1. Front-end estimation
    • Range: low and high estimates from the leading digits (round every addend down, then every addend up).
    • One-column and two-column front-end: add only the leading one or two columns, then fill with zeros.
    • Front-end with adjustment: one-column front-end, then adjust by estimating the remaining columns.
  2. Rounding
    • Rounding up and rounding down (truncating).
    • Round a 5 up: round to the nearest place, with 5 going up.
    • Round to compatible numbers: numbers that are easy to compute with, such as 24 × 26 ≈ 25 × 25 = 625.

Using a calculator

  • Arithmetic logic: the calculator performs each operation as it is entered, left to right. 3 + 4 × 5 gives 35.
  • Algebraic logic: the calculator follows the order of operations. 3 + 4 × 5 gives 23.

Chapter 4 Notes (2): Algorithms

Read Section 4.2Leads to Activities 4.3–4.8
Definition. An algorithm is a step-by-step procedure that always produces the correct answer to a given type of problem in a finite number of steps.

The rest of Chapter 4 focuses on algorithms, both standard (the ones most adults learned) and non-standard (intermediate, lattice, scaffold, and others). For every algorithm, the question is: why does it work? The answer is always place value plus the properties of the operations.

Activity 4.2: Mental Math for Subtraction and Division

Notes Notes (1)Manipulative cubes, number line

1. Compensation for subtraction: the equal-additions method

Consider 43 − 28. It would be easier to subtract 30. Count out 43 cubes: what adjustment must you make before taking anything away, if you want to take away 30 instead of 28 and get the same result? Describe the process and write it as an equation.

2. Division compensation

To divide 72 ÷ 12, count out 72 cubes, put 12 in each group, and count the groups (which approach to division is this?). Start with half as many cubes and put half as many in each group: you get the same number of groups. What did you do to the dividend and the divisor? Write it as an equation without fraction notation.

Activity 4.3: Using Base Pieces to Explore the Standard Algorithms for Addition and Subtraction

Notes Notes (2)Manipulative base ten pieces

A. Subtraction. Explore 825 − 256 with base pieces and a set model with the take-away approach.

  1. Set out the base ten pieces for 825 and sketch them.
  2. To take away 256, how many flats, longs and units must you take away? Do you have these pieces when 825 uses the fewest pieces?
  3. Take away the pieces of the subtrahend, making exchanges (1 flat for 10 longs, 1 long for 10 units) when there are not enough. You do not have to start with the units. Record each step.
  4. Perform 825 − 256 with the standard algorithm.
  5. How does the process in #3 relate to “borrowing” in the standard algorithm?

B. Addition. Explore 825 + 256 with base pieces and a set model.

  1. Set out and sketch the base pieces for 825 and 256.
  2. Combine the pieces and make exchanges to use the fewest pieces. Explain each step. What number do the pieces represent?
  3. Perform 256 + 825 with the standard algorithm.
  4. How does the process in #2 relate to “carrying” in the standard algorithm?

Activity 4.7: Understanding Long Division Using Base Pieces

Notes Notes (2); Chapter 3 Notes (4)Manipulative base ten pieces, sharing approach

Part 1: Modeling division with base pieces. Explore 653 ÷ 4 with base pieces as you did for 53 ÷ 2, using the sharing (partitive) approach.

  1. Set out the base ten pieces for 653 and sketch them.
  2. Distribute the pieces into 4 equal piles, starting with the flats. Pause after each step to sketch the pieces and explain each regrouping or exchange. Finish by explaining how the final piles give the answer.

Part 2: The standard long division algorithm.

  1. Do 653 ÷ 4 with the standard long division algorithm.
  2. Compare your base-piece work with the algorithm step by step, using colors for corresponding steps.
  3. Use multiplication and addition to check the quotient and remainder.
  4. Do 9034 ÷ 5 by distributing base pieces, then with the standard algorithm, and check with multiplication.
Dialogue to accompany 653 ÷ 4 (read it while stepping through the model)

I have 6 flats, 5 longs and 3 units to divide evenly among 4 groups.

Start with the flats. 6 flats in 4 groups: each group gets 1 flat. In the algorithm, the 1 goes above the 6 (hundreds) in the quotient. I used 4 flats and have 2 left: this is 6 − 4 in the algorithm. We “bring down” the 5: 2 flats and 5 longs, the 25 in the algorithm. Exchange the 2 flats for longs: now 25 longs.

Share the 25 longs among the 4 groups: each gets 6 longs. The 6 goes above the 5 (tens). We shared 24 longs with 1 left: 25 − 24 in the algorithm.

Bring down the 3: 1 long and 3 units, the 13 in the algorithm. Exchange the long for units: 13 units. Share them: each group gets 3 units. The 3 goes above the 3 (ones). We shared 12 with 1 left over, the remainder.

Each group has 1 flat, 6 longs and 3 units: quotient 163, remainder 1.

Activity 4.8: Additional Methods for Division

Notes Chapter 3 Notes (4): the division algorithmTool calculator method, scaffold builder

Method 1: Quotients and remainders with a calculator

Recall the division algorithm: if a and b are whole numbers and b ≠ 0, there are unique whole numbers q and r with a = bq + r and 0 ≤ r < b.

  1. Use a calculator to find the quotient and remainder of a. 1379 ÷ 87, b. 69,431 ÷ 139, c. 1,111,111 ÷ 333. Explain why your procedure works.
  2. Which conceptual approach, measurement or partitive, did you use? Explain.

Method 2: The scaffold method

This method is often a first introduction to long division; some curricula use only this method. Example from the text: 5739 ÷ 31.

  1. How do we use the scaffold to find the quotient and remainder? Why does this work?
  2. Which conceptual approach, measurement or partitive, does the scaffold method use?
  3. Use the scaffold method for a. 7496 ÷ 32 and b. 2530 ÷ 27.

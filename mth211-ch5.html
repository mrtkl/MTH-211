Chapter 5 overview

Textbook Musser et al. 10e, Ch. 5Sections 5.1 Primes, Composites and Tests for Divisibility · 5.2 Counting Factors, GCF and LCM

Big idea. Primes are the building blocks of the whole numbers under multiplication: every composite number is a product of primes in exactly one way (the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic). Divisibility tests, factor trees, the GCF and the LCM are all ways of reading that prime structure.

Why it matters for teachers. Children meet factors and multiples in arrays and skip counting, and they use the GCF and LCM later for simplifying fractions and finding common denominators. Teachers need to explain why the divisibility tests work (place value), not just state them.

How to use this file. The notes pages explain the content with manipulatives you can project. Each activity page lists the handout’s questions in order, with buttons that open the matching tool already set up.

Chapter 5 Notes (1): Primes, Composites and Divisibility

Read Section 5.1Leads to Activity 5.2

Terminology

Let a and b be whole numbers with a ≠ 0. If there is a whole number x with a · x = b, then:

  • a is a factor (or divisor) of b, and b is a multiple of a;
  • we say a divides b and write a | b. If a does not divide b, we write a ∤ b.
  • The divisors of b are all of its factors: the divisors of 12 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 12.
  • A prime is a counting number with exactly two different factors, 1 and itself: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, …
  • A composite number has more than two factors: 4, 6, 8, 9, … The number 1 is neither prime nor composite (it has only one factor).

In an array model, a | b exactly when b objects can be arranged in a rectangle with a rows and no objects left over.

Factors as rectangles

The Sieve of Eratosthenes

Choose a prime, then cross out its multiples. The numbers never crossed out are the primes.

Important theorems

The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic. Each composite number can be expressed as a product of primes in exactly one way (except for the order of the factors). Example: 360 = 2 · 2 · 2 · 3 · 3 · 5 = 2³ · 3² · 5, and every factor tree for 360 ends with these same primes.
The Prime Factor Test. To test for prime factors of a number n, one only needs to search for prime factors p with p² ≤ n. If no such prime divides n, then n is prime.
Divisibility by a product. A number is divisible by the product ab of two whole numbers a and b if it is divisible by both a and b, and a and b have only 1 as a common factor. Example: divisible by 2 and 3 ⇒ divisible by 6. But 12 is divisible by 4 and 6 and not by 24, since 4 and 6 share the factor 2.
Three divisibility facts. If a, m, n and k are whole numbers and a ≠ 0:
1. If a | m and a | n, then a | (m + n).
2. If a | m and a | n, then a | (m − n) for m > n.
3. If a | m, then a | km.

Chapter 5 Notes (2): GCF and LCM

Read Section 5.2Leads to Activity 5.3

Greatest common factor (GCF)

Definition. The greatest common factor of two (or more) nonzero whole numbers is the greatest whole number that divides each of them. We write GCF(a, b).
Example. Factors of 24: {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24}. Factors of 36: {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36}. Common factors: {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12}, so GCF(24, 36) = 12.

Finding the GCF:

  • Listing (the set intersection method in the text): list the factors of each number and take the largest number in the intersection.
  • Prime factorization method: 24 = 2³ · 3 and 36 = 2² · 3². Take each common prime to the smaller power: GCF = 2² · 3 = 12.
  • Euclidean algorithm: read about this method in the text; you will not be required to use it. GCF(a, b) = GCF(b, r), where r is the remainder of a ÷ b.

Least common multiple (LCM)

Definition. The least common multiple of two (or more) nonzero whole numbers is the smallest nonzero whole number that is a multiple of each of them. We write LCM(a, b).
Example. Multiples of 6: {6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, …}. Multiples of 8: {8, 16, 24, 32, …}. The first common multiple is 24, so LCM(6, 8) = 24.

Finding the LCM:

  • Listing (set intersection): list multiples of each and take the smallest common one.
  • Build-up method (relies on prime factorization): start with the prime factorization of one number and multiply in just the extra primes the other needs. 6 = 2 · 3; 8 = 2³ needs two more 2s: LCM = 2 · 3 · 2 · 2 = 24.
  • With prime factorizations: take each prime to the larger power: LCM(24, 36) = 2³ · 3² = 72.

Useful fact: GCF(a, b) · LCM(a, b) = a · b.

Activity 5.1: Big Bag O’ Numbers

Notes Notes (1): primes, Fundamental TheoremManipulative magic cup, number chips

Materials: a magic cup and chips numbered 1–10 (4 of each). Read the rules carefully.

  1. One player blindly draws exactly 2, 3 or 4 chips, multiplies the numbers, and tells the others only the product.
  2. The others secretly write a guess for (a) how many chips were drawn and (b) which numbers. After everyone shares, the chips are revealed.
  3. Scoring: correct number of chips, 1 point; correct numbers too, 2 more points (3 total). If nobody guesses both exactly, the drawer gets 5 points.
  4. Play passes around; play 1 round and tally scores.

Part One uses all 40 chips (1–10). Part Two uses only the prime chips. Discuss: Is there strategy? Are some guesses more likely to be right? Why does Part Two feel different?

Play the game

Analyze a product

For any product, list every way it could have been drawn from the cup. Compare the two cups, with and without the 1 chips.

Questions to ponder

  • What differences did you notice between the games? Formulate conjectures and justify them.
  • Why does it work like it does? Did your group include the chips labeled 1? What happens if 1 is included as a prime?
  • Can you explain to a peer why primes behave this way?
Show a summary

With chips 1–10, many products can be made in several ways (24 = 3 · 8 = 4 · 6 = 2 · 3 · 4 = 2 · 2 · 6 …), so guessing is a gamble and the drawer often wins. With only primes, every product breaks into primes in exactly one way, so a careful guesser can always find the chips: this is the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic. If the 1 chips are included, uniqueness fails again for the number of chips (6 = 2 · 3 = 1 · 2 · 3), which is one reason 1 is not called prime.

Activity 5.3: Greatest Common Factors and Least Common Multiples

Notes Notes (1), (2)Manipulative objects, factor trees, gears

Part 1: Finding factors

  1. List all factors of 72 systematically, pairing from the outside in (1 with 72, 2 with 36, …). When can you stop checking? Why?
  2. Katrina finds the prime factorization of 10395 with divisibility tests. a. What test first? b. What next? c. Complete the tree, describing every try.

Part 2: LCM and GCF

  1. A teacher shows only LCM(3, 10) and LCM(8, 9). What misconception might students develop? What other examples should they see?
  2. With 16 objects: a. show all the factors of 16; b. with 20 more objects, find GCF(16, 20).
  3. Sandy’s pencils (packages of 18) and erasers (packages of 24): smallest number of each so every pencil gets an eraser? How many packages? LCM or GCF?
  4. Karen’s 45 red and 30 green candies: largest number of identical gift bags using all the candy? How many of each per bag? LCM or GCF?
  5. Use the build-up or Venn diagram method: a. GCF and LCM of 54, 126; b. of 84, 98; c. of 27, 84, 98.
  6. Meshed gears with 35 and 15 teeth, stars aligned: how many turns of each until the stars align again? LCM or GCF?
  7. If a is a factor of b, what is GCF(a, b)? LCM(a, b)? Explain.
  8. When is LCM(a, b) = a × b? Explain.
